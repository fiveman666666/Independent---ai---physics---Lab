# The Beginning

## A Multi-AI Framework for Independent Scientific Cross-Checking

### Abstract

This project investigates whether multiple artificial intelligence systems can be used as independent analytical agents for scientific problem solving.

Instead of allowing the systems to communicate from the beginning, each AI receives the same scientific problem and performs its analysis independently. The individual results are recorded before any comparison takes place.

Only after this blind phase are the analyses compared.

The comparison focuses on:

* mathematical agreement and disagreement,
* differences in physical interpretation,
* calculation or reasoning errors,
* assumptions introduced by individual systems,
* and the robustness of conclusions under independent cross-checking.

The purpose of the framework is not to assume that agreement between AI systems establishes scientific truth. Multiple systems may reproduce the same error or share similar assumptions.

Instead, disagreement is treated as useful information: contradictions can identify parts of an analysis that require additional calculation, data, or human examination.

## Initial Test Case

The first experimental application uses astronomical galaxy data.

A mathematical relationship is specified before the data comparison. Independent analyses then examine its mathematical consequences and test whether those consequences are supported by observational data.

The results of the separate analyses are subsequently compared.

## Objective

The long-term objective is to develop a reproducible workflow in which scientific hypotheses can pass through several independent analytical paths before a combined assessment is produced.

The project therefore follows a simple principle:

**Independent analysis → comparison → contradiction detection → verification.**

This repository documents the development, selected experiments, datasets, calculations, and results of **The Beginning**.

Mathematical Model
Base Relation

The initial test model is defined by:

a_tot = a_N + p × sqrt(a_N × a_0)

where:

a_N = G × M / r²

is the Newtonian acceleration generated by the baryonic mass M, and

a_tot = v² / r

is the total acceleration inferred from the observed rotational velocity v.

Here, a_0 is a fixed acceleration scale and p is a dimensionless parameter to be determined from observational data.

Low-Acceleration Limit

For the low-acceleration regime:

a_N << a_0

the square-root contribution can dominate, giving approximately:

a_tot ≈ p × sqrt(a_N × a_0)

Substituting:

a_N = G × M / r²

and:

a_tot = v² / r

gives:

v² / r = p × sqrt[(G × M / r²) × a_0]

For r > 0, this reduces to:

v² = p × sqrt(G × M × a_0)

Squaring both sides gives:

v⁴ = p² × G × M × a_0

Testable Consequences

The explicit dependence on radius disappears in this asymptotic limit.

The model therefore predicts two testable consequences in the low-acceleration regime:

An asymptotically radius-independent rotational velocity, corresponding to a flat rotation curve.
A fourth-power velocity relation:

v⁴ ∝ M

for constant p and a_0.

These consequences provide direct targets for comparison with observational galaxy data.

The parameter p must be estimated from the data rather than assumed. The stability of p across different galaxies constitutes an additional empirical test of the model.

Agreement with these mathematical consequences alone does not establish the physical origin of the relation; it provides predictions that can be tested against observational data.
