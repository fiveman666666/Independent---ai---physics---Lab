The Beginning

A Multi-AI Framework for Independent Scientific Cross-Checking.

Abstract
This project investigates wehther multible artiificial intelligence system can be used as independent analytical agents forscientific problem solving.
Instead of allowing the systems to communicate from the beginning, each AI receives the same scientific problem and performs its analysis independently. The individual results are recorded before any comparison takes place.

Only after this blind phase are the analyses compared.

The comparison focuses on

mathematical agreement and disagreement,
differences in physical interpretatio,
calculation or reasoning errors,
assumptions introduced by individual sistems,
and the robustness of conclusions under independent cross-checking.

The purpose of the framework is not to assume that agreement between AI systems establishes scientific truth. Multiple systems may reproduce the same error or share similar assumptions.

Instead, disagreement is treated as useful information: contradictions can identify parts of an analysis that require additional calculation, data, or human examination.

Initial Test Casse

The first experimental application uses astronomikal galaxy data.

A mathematical relationship is specified before the data comparison. Independent analyses then examine its mathematical consequences and test whether those consequences are supported by the observational data.

The results of the separate analyses are subsequently compored.

Objective

The long-term objective is to develop a reproducible workflow in which scientific hypotheses can pass through several independent analytical paths before a combined assessment is produced.

The project therefore follows a simple principle:

Independent analysis → comparison → contradiction detection → verification.

This repositori documents the development, selected experiments, datasets, calculations and results of The Beginning.

## Mathematical Model

### Base Relation

The initial test model is defined by:

**a_tot = a_N + p × sqrt(a_N × a_0)**

where:

**a_N = G × M / r²**

is the Newtonian acceleration generated by the baryonic mass M, and

**a_tot = v² / r**

is the total acceleration inferred from the observed rotational velocity v.

Here, a_0 is a fixed acceleration scale and p is a dimensionless parameter to be determined from observational data.

### Low-Acceleration Limit

For the low-acceleration regime:

**a_N << a_0**

the square-root contribution can dominate, giving approximately:

**a_tot ≈ p × sqrt(a_N × a_0)**

Substituting:

**a_N = G × M / r²**

and:

**a_tot = v² / r**

gives:

**v² / r = p × sqrt[(G × M / r²) × a_0]**

For r > 0:

**v² = p × sqrt(G × M × a_0)**

Squaring both sides gives:

**v⁴ = p² × G × M × a_0**

## Testable Consequences

The explicit dependence on radius disappears in this asymptotic limit.

The model therefore predicts two testable consequences in the low-acceleration regime:

1. An asymptotically radius-independent rotational velocity, corresponding to a flat rotation curve.

2. A fourth-power velocity relation:

**v⁴ ∝ M**

for constant p and a_0.

These consequences provide direct targets for comparison with observational galaxy data.

The parameter p must be estimated from the data rather than assumed. The stability of p across different galaxies constitutes an additional empirical test of the model.

Agreement with these mathematical consequences alone does not establish the physical origin of the relation; it provides predictions that can be tested against observational data.

# Data and First Results

## Dataset

The first empirical test uses galaxy rotation-curve data from the SPARC dataset.

The working dataset contained:

**Total measurement points: 3,391**

Two measurement points were excluded because the calculated baryonic squared velocity contribution was negative:

* UGC01281, R = 0.08 kpc, V_gas = -5.85 km/s, V_disk = 1.50 km/s, V_bul = 0.00 km/s, V_bar² = -33.0975

* UGC01281, R = 0.23 kpc, V_gas = -5.85 km/s, V_disk = 3.55 km/s, V_bul = 0.00 km/s, V_bar² = -27.9212

This leaves:

**Measurement points used: 3,389**

## Tested Relation

The tested relation is:

**a_tot = a_N + p × sqrt(a_N × a_0)**

The purpose of the first test was to estimate the dimensionless parameter p from the observational data using different fitting approaches.

## First Results

Three fitting approaches produced the following global estimates:

**Unweighted acceleration fit: p = 0.609**

**Error-weighted acceleration fit: p = 0.714**

**Velocity fit: p = 0.775**

The estimated value of p therefore depends on the fitting method.

This difference is part of the result and requires further investigation. At this stage, the analysis does not establish that p is a universal constant.

Instead, these measurements provide a quantitative starting point for further tests.

## Single-Galaxy Check

NGC 3198 was also examined separately using its 43-point rotation curve.

The analysis indicated that the additional square-root term can improve the description relative to the purely baryonic prediction.

A successful fit to an individual galaxy, however, is not sufficient to establish a universal relation.

## Next Tests

The next stage is to investigate:

* how strongly p varies from galaxy to galaxy,
* whether this variation can be explained by observational uncertainties,
* whether one global p can adequately describe multiple galaxies,
* how the model compares with alternative relations,
* and whether the apparent improvement survives independent statistical cross-checking.

These results should therefore be regarded as first empirical measurements of the model's behaviour, not as confirmation of the model.

# Formal Calculation Procedure

## 1. Input Quantities

For each radial measurement point, the analysis uses the observed rotational velocity and the available baryonic velocity components:

**V_obs, V_gas, V_disk, V_bul, R**

where R is the galactocentric radius.

## 2. Baryonic Contribution

The total baryonic squared velocity contribution is calculated as:

**V_bar² = V_gas × |V_gas| + V_disk² + V_bul²**

The signed gas contribution is retained.

Only measurement points satisfying:

**V_bar² > 0**

are used in the acceleration analysis.

## 3. Unit Conversion

Radius is converted from kiloparsecs to metres:

**r = R × 3.085677581 × 10¹⁹ m**

Velocity is converted from kilometres per second to metres per second:

**V = V(km/s) × 1000**

## 4. Observed Acceleration

For each accepted measurement point:

**a_obs = V_obs² / r**

## 5. Newtonian Baryonic Acceleration

The baryonic Newtonian acceleration is calculated from:

**a_N = (V_bar² × 10⁶) / r**

where the factor 10⁶ converts (km/s)² to (m/s)².

## 6. Model Transformation

The tested model is:

**a_model = a_N + p × sqrt(a_N × a_0)**

Define:

**X = sqrt(a_N × a_0)**

and:

**Y = a_obs - a_N**

The relation can then be written as:

**Y = p × X**

This converts the acceleration test into a one-parameter regression through the origin.

## 7. Unweighted Acceleration Fit

The unweighted value of p is obtained by minimizing:

**Σ(Y_i - p × X_i)²**

The analytical least-squares solution is:

**p = Σ(X_i × Y_i) / Σ(X_i²)**

For the current dataset, this procedure produced:

**p = 0.609**

## 8. Error-Weighted Acceleration Fit

For the weighted analysis, each measurement point receives a statistical weight based on its observational uncertainty.

The fit minimizes:

**Σ[w_i × (Y_i - p × X_i)²]**

with:

**w_i = 1 / σ_i²**

The corresponding solution is:

**p = Σ(w_i × X_i × Y_i) / Σ(w_i × X_i²)**

For the current dataset, the error-weighted fit produced:

**p = 0.714**

The exact uncertainty definition and propagation used for the weighting must be documented alongside the dataset and analysis code for full reproducibility.

## 9. Velocity-Space Fit

The model acceleration is converted into a predicted rotational velocity:

**V_model = sqrt[(a_N + p × sqrt(a_N × a_0)) × r]**

The parameter p is varied to minimize the residuals between:

**V_obs**

and:

**V_model**

over the accepted measurement points.

For the current dataset, the velocity-space fit produced:

**p = 0.775**

## 10. Reproducibility

The three estimates of p originate from different fitting criteria and must therefore be reported separately.

No single value should be selected solely because it produces the preferred result.

All constants, exclusions, uncertainty definitions, weighting rules and fitting criteria should be documented so that the calculation can be independently reproduced.

The current calculation therefore provides three empirical estimates:

**p = 0.609 — unweighted acceleration fit**

**p = 0.714 — error-weighted acceleration fit**

**p = 0.775 — velocity-space fit**

# Scientific Framework

## 1. Hypothesis

The working hypothesis is that the additional acceleration term

**p × sqrt(a_N × a_0)**

captures a systematic relationship between baryonic acceleration and observed rotational acceleration in galaxy rotation curves.

If p represents a physically meaningful common parameter, independently analysed galaxies should yield mutually consistent values of p within observational and modelling uncertainties.

## 2. Null Hypothesis

The null hypothesis is that no universal value of p is required by the observational data.

Under this hypothesis, apparent improvements may result from additional fitting freedom, observational uncertainties, galaxy-specific properties, correlations within the dataset, or the mathematical flexibility of the chosen relation.

The model must therefore be compared against the null hypothesis and appropriate alternative models.

## 3. Falsifiability

The proposed relation is treated as falsifiable.

Evidence against a universal interpretation would include:

* statistically incompatible values of p across galaxies,
* systematic residual patterns,
* failure on independent galaxy samples,
* failure to outperform appropriate baseline models after accounting for model complexity,
* or predictions inconsistent with observations.

A failed prediction is considered information about the model rather than something to be removed from the analysis.

## 4. Limitations

The current results are preliminary.

The fitted values of p depend on the chosen fitting method, and the present analysis does not establish a new physical law or identify the physical origin of the observed relation.

Possible systematic effects include observational uncertainties, assumptions in the baryonic decomposition, stellar mass-to-light ratios, distance and inclination uncertainties, correlations between measurements from the same galaxy, and sample-selection effects.

Independent reproduction, statistical model comparison, sensitivity tests, and validation on data not used during model development are required before stronger conclusions can be justified.
